7 Best AI Tools for Musicians in 2026 (Compose, Mix & Master Smarter)

Updated November 2026 · 9 min read · Category: Music

AI has moved from a novelty in music to a working part of the studio: it can sketch a chord progression when you are stuck, separate a vocal from a finished mix, master a track overnight, and build a practice loop around the exact bar you keep flubbing. The tools below are not replacements for musicianship — they are time-savers that handle the repetitive parts so you can spend your hours on the music itself. This guide compares seven options — LANDR, Moises, AIVA, BandLab, Soundraw, iZotope Ozone, and LALAL.AI — so you can pick the ones that fit your workflow.

Important: check the licensing terms before you release anything AI-assisted commercially. Some tools grant full commercial rights, others limit use to demos or require a paid tier — the fine print matters more than the feature list.

What to look for in an AI tool for musicians

  • Sound quality, not just features: A stem separator that leaves artefacts or a mastering engine that squashes dynamics is worse than no tool at all. Test every tool on your own recordings, not on its demo audio.
  • Commercial rights: Know exactly what you own. Some generators keep a share of the rights or forbid commercial use on free tiers — fine for a sketch, fatal for a release.
  • Workflow fit: The best tool is the one that fits where you already work — a plugin inside your DAW beats a separate website you have to remember to open.
  • Control, not just automation: One-click results are great for demos, but for finished work you need to adjust the mix, the key, the tempo, and the arrangement yourself.
  • Export options: Stems, MIDI, and lossless WAV exports matter. A tool that only exports compressed MP3 will limit everything downstream.

1. LANDR

LANDR is the best-known AI mastering service: upload a mix and its engine analyses the track and applies EQ, compression, and loudness processing, with style presets tuned for genres from hip-hop to classical. Best for independent artists who need release-ready masters without hiring a mastering engineer for every single. Pricing starts with a free preview tier; unlimited mastering is a subscription — check current pricing.

Beyond mastering, LANDR now bundles distribution, samples, and collaboration tools, so it can carry a track from finished mix to streaming. The masters are solid for streaming loudness targets, though a human engineer still wins on delicate, dynamic material.

2. Moises

Moises separates a finished song into stems — vocals, drums, bass, guitar, piano — and adds practice tools like tempo change, pitch shift, and a smart metronome. Best for practising musicians, cover artists, and producers who want to learn from or remix existing recordings. Free tier available; premium unlocks higher-quality separation and more uploads.

The practice workflow is the standout: slow a solo down without changing pitch, mute the guitar and play along, or loop a tricky four bars until it clicks. Separation quality is strong on clean recordings and weaker on dense, heavily compressed mixes.

3. AIVA

AIVA composes original music in styles from cinematic orchestral to electronic, generating full arrangements from a style preset or a reference influence. Best for composers scoring film, games, or video who need a strong starting draft or a full backing track fast. Free tier available for non-commercial use; paid plans unlock full copyright ownership.

Unlike pure text-to-music generators, AIVA outputs editable MIDI, so you can take its draft into your DAW, re-voice the strings, and make it yours. That editability is what makes it a composition tool rather than a jukebox.

4. BandLab

BandLab is a free, cloud-based DAW with built-in AI mastering, a huge loop library, and social collaboration — you can record, mix, master, and share a track from your phone. Best for beginners, bedroom producers, and collaborators working across devices. Free, with optional paid extras.

The price is the headline: a genuinely capable multitrack studio costs nothing. The AI mastering is a one-click finish that is good enough for demos and social posts, and the mobile app makes capturing ideas anywhere effortless.

5. Soundraw

Soundraw generates royalty-free music by letting you pick mood, genre, length, and instruments, then produces editable tracks you can fine-tune section by section. Best for content creators, video producers, and musicians who need custom backing music without licensing headaches. Subscription pricing — check current pricing.

The section editor is the difference-maker: if the chorus is too busy or the intro too long, you adjust that part without regenerating the whole track. The output is production music, not art — but for soundtracks and social video, that is exactly the job.

6. iZotope Ozone

iZotope Ozone is a professional mastering suite whose AI assistant listens to your track and builds a starting mastering chain — EQ, dynamics, maximiser — which you then refine with full manual control. Best for producers and engineers who want AI speed with professional-grade control. Paid plugin, with a free trial — check current pricing.

Ozone's strength is that the AI is a starting point, not a black box: every suggestion is an adjustable module, and the reference-matching feature lets you aim your master at a commercial track you admire. It rewards users who already understand mastering and want to move faster.

7. LALAL.AI

LALAL.AI is a dedicated stem-separation service that splits audio into vocals and instruments — or into ten stems, including drums, bass, piano, and synth — using a neural network trained specifically for clean separation. Best for remixers, DJs, and producers who need the cleanest possible vocal or instrumental extraction. Pay-per-minute or subscription pricing — check current pricing.

Separation quality is its entire pitch, and on most material it is a step cleaner than general-purpose tools, with fewer artefacts on vocals. There are no practice or composition features — it does one job, and does it very well.

Which one should you pick?

  • You are releasing your own songs: LANDR for mastering and distribution, with Ozone if you want to master inside your DAW.
  • You practise, cover, or learn songs: Moises — the tempo, pitch, and stem-muting tools are built for exactly that.
  • You compose for film, games, or video: AIVA for editable drafts, or Soundraw when you need finished royalty-free backing tracks.
  • You are starting out or producing on your phone: BandLab — a free DAW with mastering built in is hard to beat.
  • You remix or DJ: LALAL.AI for the cleanest stems to work with.

Start with the free tiers — BandLab and Moises cost nothing to try — and test every tool on your own recordings before committing to a subscription.

FAQ

Can AI tools replace a music producer or mastering engineer?

No. AI handles routine, repeatable work — a first master, a stem split, a draft arrangement — very well. Taste, arrangement decisions, and the final 10% of a mix still need human ears, and the best results come from using AI as a fast assistant, not a replacement.

Who owns music created with AI tools?

It depends on the tool and the plan. AIVA and Soundraw grant commercial rights on paid plans, while free tiers often limit use to non-commercial projects. Always read the licence for your specific plan before releasing a track.

What is the best free AI tool for musicians?

BandLab is the strongest free option — a full DAW, loops, collaboration, and AI mastering at no cost. Moises also has a genuinely useful free tier for stem separation and practice.

Is AI mastering good enough for Spotify and Apple Music?

For most independent releases, yes — LANDR and Ozone produce masters that meet streaming loudness standards. For dynamic, acoustic, or classical material, a human mastering engineer will usually do better.

Can I use AI-separated stems commercially?

Separating stems from a copyrighted song does not give you rights to that song. You can use stems for practice, study, and private remixes, but releasing a remix commercially requires permission from the rights holders.
