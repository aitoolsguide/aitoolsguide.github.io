7 Best AI Tools for Writers and Authors in 2026 (Fiction, Nonfiction & Editing)

Updated October 2026 · 8 min read · Category: Writing

Every writer knows the two hard parts of a book: getting the story out of your head, and getting the draft clean enough for another human to read. AI tools cannot supply the first part — the ideas, the lived experience, and the voice are yours — but in 2026 they are genuinely useful for the work around it: breaking a plot block, drafting a scene you can rewrite, keeping a long manuscript consistent, and catching the editing problems your own eyes have learned to skip. The seven tools below are split by job on purpose. Plotting tools, drafting assistants, and editing tools solve different problems, and the right combination depends on whether you are writing a novel, a memoir, a nonfiction book, or commercial copy.

Quick Verdict

Short version: Claude as the best all-round drafting and editing partner for long manuscripts, Sudowrite for fiction writers blocked on scenes and descriptions, Novelcrafter for planning and keeping a novel's world consistent, Jasper for nonfiction authors and writers who also produce marketing copy, Grammarly for everyday grammar and clarity checks, ProWritingAid for deep style analysis and self-editing, and AutoCrit for manuscript-level fiction editing against published writing patterns.

The 7 Tools, Reviewed

1. Claude — Best All-Round Drafting and Editing Partner

Claude is the tool many working writers now keep open beside their manuscript. Its strength is long-form handling: paste in a chapter, and it can summarise what happens, flag where the pacing drags, suggest a tighter rewrite of a flat passage, or check that a character's motivation stays consistent with an earlier scene. It is equally useful for nonfiction — restructuring a messy chapter outline, simplifying a technical explanation, or turning interview notes into a first draft. The tone of its edits tends to be restrained rather than flashy, which matters when you are protecting your own voice. Treat it as a sharp first reader who never gets tired, not as a ghostwriter: the best results come when you bring it your draft and ask it to improve specific things.

Free plan: Free tier with daily limits; Pro (around $20/month) for heavier daily writing use.

Strengths: Excellent with long chapters, thoughtful editing suggestions, natural rewrite tone.

Best for: Authors and nonfiction writers who want one strong partner for drafting, feedback, and revision.

Weakness: It is a general chatbot, not a manuscript manager — it will not organise your novel's chapters or worldbuilding for you.

2. Sudowrite — Best for Fiction Writers and Scene Blocks

Sudowrite is built specifically for fiction, and it shows. Its "Write" feature continues a scene in your style when you are stuck, "Describe" generates sensory description for a setting or character, "Brainstorm" produces plot twists, character names, and dialogue options, and "Twist" suggests a new direction when a scene goes flat. Novelists use it less to write whole chapters and more to get past the exact moment where writing stalls: the next paragraph, the description of a room, the line of dialogue that makes a character come alive. Because it is trained with fiction in mind, its continuations read more like story prose and less like an essay, which is the usual failure mode of general chatbots for fiction.

Free plan: No permanent free tier; paid plans with a trial, priced by monthly word credits.

Strengths: Fiction-first features, good scene continuation, excellent for beating writer's block.

Best for: Novelists and short-story writers who stall mid-scene and need a push, not a replacement.

Weakness: Word-credit pricing can get expensive for heavy users; output still needs your rewrite to sound like you.

3. Novelcrafter — Best for Planning and Novel Consistency

Novelcrafter combines a novel-planning workspace with AI assistance: the codex tracks characters, locations, plot threads, and worldbuilding, while the AI writes with that codex in view, so it is far less likely to rename a character or forget a plot point from chapter three. Writers plan scenes on beats and acts, then draft inside the same tool, with word counts and progress tracking built in. For long novels, series, and fantasy or historical fiction with complicated worlds, this consistency is the main selling point — a general chatbot forgets your world between sessions, while Novelcrafter is designed to remember it. The AI can also generate a plot outline from a premise, or summarise a chapter back to you so you can check the story still makes sense.

Free plan: Free tier for planning and writing basics; paid plans add AI generation and the full codex features.

Strengths: Codex keeps characters and worldbuilding consistent, planning plus drafting in one place, built for long novels.

Best for: Novelists writing long or series fiction with large casts, complex plots, or detailed worlds.

Weakness: The full workflow takes time to learn; short-story writers will not need most of what it offers.

4. Jasper — Best for Nonfiction Authors and Marketing Copy

Jasper is aimed at writers who produce a high volume of professional content: nonfiction authors writing companion articles, newsletter writers, and authors who also handle their own book marketing. It works from brand voice settings and templates for blog posts, emails, social posts, ad copy, and book descriptions, so output stays consistent across a whole launch campaign. A nonfiction author can draft a chapter summary, turn it into a newsletter, and then into a week of social posts without starting each piece from scratch. It is less suited to literary fiction — its house style leans commercial and clean — but for authors who treat writing as a business as well as a craft, that commercial polish is the point.

Free plan: No permanent free tier; paid plans with a trial, priced per month.

Strengths: Strong templates and brand voice, excellent for marketing and nonfiction content, team-friendly.

Best for: Nonfiction authors, newsletter writers, and authors running their own book marketing.

Weakness: The commercial tone needs softening for literary work; the price is aimed at professional users.

5. Grammarly — Best for Everyday Grammar and Clarity

Grammarly is the everyday safety net: it catches grammar, spelling, and punctuation errors in real time, inside the word processor, email, or browser where you write. Beyond corrections, it flags wordy sentences, weak word choices, and tone mismatches — a cover letter that reads as too casual, or a query letter that reads as too stiff. For authors, its value is in the early passes: run a chapter through Grammarly and the mechanical errors disappear, leaving your revision time for story and style instead of commas. It will not fix a weak plot or a flat character, and it should never be the only edit a manuscript gets, but no manuscript should reach a beta reader with errors Grammarly would have caught.

Free plan: Free tier covers grammar and spelling; Premium adds clarity, tone, and full-sentence rewrites.

Strengths: Works everywhere you write, instant corrections, useful tone detection.

Best for: Every writer, as the first editing pass on any draft, query, or proposal.

Weakness: Surface-level only — it edits sentences, not stories.

6. ProWritingAid — Best for Deep Style Analysis and Self-Editing

ProWritingAid is the self-editing tool for writers who want to understand why their prose is not working. Its reports cover overused words, sentence-length variety, passive voice, dialogue tags, pacing, repeated sentence starts, and readability — the patterns that make prose feel flat even when every sentence is grammatically correct. Running a chapter through its reports before sending it to an editor regularly cuts editing costs, because the mechanical style problems are already fixed. It integrates with Scrivener, Word, and Google Docs, so the analysis happens inside the manuscript rather than in a separate tool. For writers preparing a manuscript for submission, it is the closest thing to a line editor that runs on demand.

Free plan: Free tier with limited reports; Premium (around $10/month billed annually) unlocks the full report set.

Strengths: Deep, writer-focused reports, excellent for self-editing, integrates with Scrivener and Word.

Best for: Authors self-editing a manuscript before beta readers or a professional editor.

Weakness: The volume of reports can overwhelm new writers — focus on two or three reports per pass, not all of them.

7. AutoCrit — Best for Manuscript-Level Fiction Editing

AutoCrit analyses a full manuscript against patterns found in published fiction: pacing, dialogue balance, showing versus telling, repetition, strong versus weak word choices, and sentence variety across the whole book rather than one chapter at a time. Where Grammarly checks sentences and ProWritingAid checks style, AutoCrit checks whether the manuscript behaves like a published novel. Fiction writers use it for the final self-edit before querying agents or self-publishing, and its comparison reports show how a manuscript's dialogue and pacing stack up against genre benchmarks. It is a specialised tool, and that is its strength — it does one job, for one audience, unusually well.

Free plan: No permanent free tier; paid plans with a trial, priced per month.

Strengths: Whole-manuscript fiction analysis, genre benchmarks, strong on pacing and dialogue balance.

Best for: Fiction authors doing a final self-edit before submission or self-publishing.

Weakness: Fiction-only — nonfiction writers will get little from it, and it does not replace a human developmental editor.

Which One Should You Pick?

Writing a novel? Novelcrafter for planning, Sudowrite when you stall, and AutoCrit for the final self-edit. Writing nonfiction or a memoir? Claude for drafting and restructuring, plus ProWritingAid for the style pass. Writing for a living? Jasper for volume and marketing, Grammarly for the everyday check on everything you send. On a tight budget? Claude's free tier and Grammarly's free tier together cover drafting help and error-checking at no cost.

Tips for Writers Using AI

Keep the ideas yours. AI is strongest as a drafting assistant and editor; a book built entirely from AI output reads like one, and readers notice. Rewrite every AI passage in your voice. If a sentence does not sound like something you would write, cut it or rewrite it until it does. Use different tools for different passes. Draft with one tool, check style with another, and proofread with a third — each pass catches what the last one missed. Check disclosure rules before you submit. Some publishers, agents, and competitions ask whether AI was used; know the rules for your market and be honest about your process. Note that prices change often — check each tool's current pricing page before committing.

FAQ

Can I write a whole book with AI?

Technically yes, but it is rarely a good idea. AI-written books tend to be generic, repetitive, and inconsistent over a full manuscript, and many publishers and retailers now ask about AI use. The strongest use of AI is as an assistant for drafting, brainstorming, and editing — with the author's ideas and voice in charge.

Which AI tool is best for fiction writing?

Sudowrite for beating scene-level blocks and Novelcrafter for planning a long novel. For editing and feedback on completed chapters, Claude is the strongest general partner.

Will using AI tools hurt my chances with agents or publishers?

Using AI for brainstorming and editing is widely accepted; submitting a book written entirely by AI is not, and most agents expect the work to be the author's own. Check the submission guidelines of any agent or publisher you approach, and disclose AI use if they ask.

Can AI tools replace a human editor?

No. AI tools are excellent for self-editing passes and catching mechanical problems, but a human developmental editor understands story, character, and audience in ways no tool can. Use AI to make the manuscript as clean as possible before it reaches a human editor — not instead of one.

Are AI writing tools safe for unpublished manuscripts?

Check each tool's privacy policy before uploading a full manuscript. Reputable writing tools state that your work remains yours and is not used for training, but terms vary — and free consumer chatbots may have different terms from paid writing tools. When in doubt, work chapter by chapter and keep backups of your own files.
